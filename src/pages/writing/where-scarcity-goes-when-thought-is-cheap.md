The people building machine intelligence increasingly say where they think it leads: to a world in which robots and software produce more than anyone can consume. Their argument is that scarcity then ends, and that prices, money and the need to economise end with it. In this article I argue that the argument mistakes a relation for a property. Scarcity is a relation between what people want and what they can have, so abundance moves it and does not end it. First I ask what stays scarce when thought becomes cheap. Then I ask who decides how the scarce things are used, and why that takes prices and money. Finally I turn to trust: why money rests on promises, how a base can be built that rests on none, and what may come once machines begin to build on each other's work.

## 1. A world without money

Iain M. Banks published the Culture books between 1987 and 2012. The Culture is a civilisation that has left scarcity behind. Its machines make whatever anyone asks for, and nobody has to work. Artificial Minds of almost unlimited ability run its ships and habitats. With today's pace of development, that scenario seems to be approaching ever faster. It is also a system without money. The first novel calls money a clumsy way of rationing, made pointless once production outran every demand.

The books are favourites of the people now building such machines. SpaceX's rocket-recovery ships carry the names of Culture spacecraft, a meeting room at DeepMind is named after Banks, and he has been called one of the technology industry's favourite writers ([The Economist](https://econ.st/2HqNLbG)). For at least one of them the novels are more than entertainment. He has called them his best picture of the future that intelligent machines could bring ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/12/15/billionaire-elon-musk-say-that-money-will-disappear-in-the-future-as-ai-makes-work-and-salaries-irrelevant-sorry-six-figure-earners/)). The forecast taken from Banks runs as follows. If machines and software agents can produce more goods and services than anyone can consume, nothing is left to economise. Prices would fall toward zero, work would become a hobby, and money would be a relic.

A world in which everything is given is a world in which nothing has to be given up. No good is preferred to another for want of means, so there is no order among desirable things and no reason to do one thing before another.

Economics has an older view of what that would mean, and it runs the other way. Robbins (1932) defined the subject as the study of behaviour where ends are ranked and the means to reach them are scarce and have alternative uses. Mises opened *Human Action* (1949) with the same thought in its simplest form: to act is to choose, to prefer one state of affairs and set another aside. On that view the world just described is not a world of plenty. It is a world of beings who do not choose. Wherever someone still prefers anything to anything else, and cannot have both at once, something is scarce.

From that it follows that the forecast cannot be true in its strict form. Something always stays scarce, and the first task is to say what.

## 2. Scarcity is a relation: the land of Cockaigne

To see why, start with what scarcity is. It is a relation between wants and means, not a property of things, so abundance moves it without ending it. Menger (1871) defined an economic good as one whose available quantity falls short of requirements. Machines can push many goods across that line. They cannot abolish the line.

The oldest picture of the other side of the line is the land of Cockaigne. In the medieval poems its rivers run with wine, its houses are roofed with cakes, and roast fowl fly into open mouths. Bruegel painted it in 1567: a clerk, a peasant and a soldier asleep on the ground beneath a laden table. It was a peasant's dream, and what it abolished was labour.

Mises (1949) used it as a test case and found what the dream leaves standing. Even in a land of Cockaigne, where every material want is met, a person must still decide what to do first. Some satisfactions exclude each other and cannot be enjoyed in the same hour.

Life is finite and satisfactions are sequenced, so time is economised whatever else is free. The only exit Mises allows is immortality with eternal youth, and even then the ordering of incompatible pleasures remains. One could argue that a digital identity may last forever. Two things have to be kept apart, though. Lasting forever is not the same as doing everything at once: a mind that never dies still acts in sequence, and every hour it runs uses energy and hardware that could have served something else. And the wants the machines serve are still those of people, whose hours are numbered.

Time preference follows from that. A satisfaction now is ranked above the same satisfaction later, because the later one costs waiting and waiting uses up the one stock that cannot be replenished. Interest is the price at which present goods trade against future ones. It would exist in Cockaigne too, as soon as anyone there promised anything to anyone. Bruegel's sleepers cannot feast while they sleep.

Machine intelligence is the nearest thing to Cockaigne yet built or anticipated, and only for one class of goods: those made of thought. The question is where scarcity goes when thought is cheap.

## 3. Where scarcity goes

When one factor of production becomes cheap to reproduce, income moves to the factors that complement it and cannot be reproduced. Three older literatures describe the same movement from different sides.

Hirsch (1976) separated material goods, which technology can multiply, from positional goods, whose value lies in relative standing. Baumol (1967) showed that services which need a person's hour grow dearer as everything else grows cheaper. George (1879) argued that land rent absorbs the gains of everyone else's productivity, because land is fixed.

All three describe value retreating into what cannot be reproduced: rank, another person's hour, a location. Rank is scarce by definition, which is why status competition survives any level of output. A house with a view is preferred to one without, however many houses are built. The argument is ordinal. It needs only that people rank outcomes, never that anyone measures them.

Böhm-Bawerk (1889) supplies the classification. Land and labour are original factors. Capital goods are produced, so their prices tend toward cost. If machines make substitutes for labour reproducible, rents shift to the factors that stay original.

The evidence predates the machines. The rise in the net capital share of advanced economies since 1950 is largely housing (Rognlie, 2015), and the long rise in house prices is largely land (Knoll, Schularick and Steger, 2017). Software became abundant, and the returns went to whoever owned its scarce complement.

So the list of what stays scarce is short. It holds time and attention, land and location, energy at a given place and hour, and rank. Two newer items join it. One is proof of origin: when text, images and voices can be synthesised without limit, evidence that a thing existed before a certain moment, or came from a certain person, becomes a good in its own right. The other is a unit whose quantity is fixed by a rule that nobody can change. Like land, more of it cannot be made. Land is limited by nature and the unit by a rule.

## 4. What creates value, and who decides

The abundance forecast is at bottom an argument from cost: what costs nothing to make will be worth nothing. For goods that can be reproduced, that is right. For the scarce things just listed it is not, because their value never came from cost.

Value runs from ends to means, never the other way. A machine, a mine or an hour of work has value only because the thing it helps to produce is wanted. Economists call this the law of imputation. Costs do not escape it. The cost of a factor is the value of what it could have produced elsewhere, which is imputed in the same way. Energy and cost follow price.

A clean example is any asset whose new units are awarded, on a fixed schedule, to whoever spends the most computation competing for them. Let N be the number of awards per year, R the units per award, P the price of a unit, c the price of electricity per kilowatt-hour and φ the share of power in marginal cost. Competitors enter until the cost of competing equals the revenue, so annual energy use is:

```text
E* = φ × N × R × P / c    (kWh per year)
```

The formula answers a common intuition, which is that better machines should make such an asset cheaper to produce or less hungry for energy. They do neither, and that is why machine efficiency does not appear in it. Energy use follows revenue: halve the price and it halves. The energy spent does not give the unit its value. The value of the unit decides how much energy is spent.

The slogan that such an asset is "stored energy" is therefore a cost theory of value with joules in place of labour hours. Its lineage is Ricardo and Marx, then Henry Ford's energy dollar of 1921, Soddy's energy economics and the Technocrats' energy certificates. It is not Menger's. A kilowatt-hour has no single value in any case. At noon in a solar field it is close to worthless, and on a windless winter evening in a city it is worth a great deal.

If value comes from what people want and cannot all have, someone has to decide how the scarce things are used. There are two candidates: a planner, or prices. The Culture chooses the planner. Its Minds are intelligent enough to allocate everything directly, so prices are not needed and money withers.

Four results stand against that choice. The knowledge an economy runs on is dispersed, local and often tacit (Hayek, 1945). Individual rankings cannot, under a few mild conditions, be combined into a consistent social ranking without a dictator (Arrow, 1951). People's values change with the experiences they choose, so there is no fixed target to aim at (Paul, 2014). And without exchange between owners there are no prices for capital goods, and without prices no plan can be compared with another (Mises, 1920).

The last bears hardest on machines. The problem is the absence of the numbers, not the difficulty of processing them. More intelligence processes given data faster. It does not create the bids of rival owners risking their own property, and those bids are what a price is.

The natural reply is that the machine could simulate the market. But preferences are not a dataset waiting to be read. They show themselves only in choice (Rothbard, 1956). A simulated bid risks nothing, and it is the risk of loss that makes a bid informative. There will be markets.

A machine given a fixed objective is the planner's error in silicon. The question is then who sets the objective, and the four results say that no procedure yields one that is right for everyone. The designs that take this seriously keep the machine uncertain about what people want and have it learn from what they do (Russell, 2019).

Nor does a world of software agents escape the argument. It sharpens it. Billions of agents will compete for electricity and computing capacity, both of which have other uses. Deciding which task gets the next kilowatt-hour is a calculation problem, and it is solved the old way, by prices. An agentic economy needs more prices than a human one, not fewer. Smart investors already look at the electrical infrastructure companies that make this possible.

## 5. Whose promise is money?

Prices have to be stated in something, and that something is money. Money is the most saleable good: the one thing everyone accepts because everyone else does. In barter, a trader who cannot find a direct match accepts a good that is easier to sell on than his own. The more people do this with one good, the easier that good becomes to sell, until it is wanted for that reason alone.

The abundance forecast sees only one of money's jobs. It assumes money exists to buy produced goods, and that job does shrink as goods get cheap. But money also allocates whatever stays scarce, and it carries decisions through time. It is easy to forget that money is meant to be a store of value and not only a medium of exchange. Neither of those jobs depends on goods being dear.

The second of those jobs is the one Section 2 prepared. Present goods trade against future goods through money, so it is the medium in which time preference is expressed. A person who holds money is keeping a decision open.

Holding money through time raises the question this section is named for, because most of what people call money is a promise. A bank deposit is the bank's promise to pay, and it passes as money for as long as nobody doubts it. Holmström (2015) calls this debt that nobody examines. Every monetary system is therefore a hierarchy, with an asset at the base that is nobody's liability and layers of promises above it. The promises are convenient and usually sound. They are also where trust enters, and where it can fail.

In digital form the promises go all the way down. A balance at a bank, a central bank's digital currency and a dollar token from a private issuer are all entries that someone keeps and could change. The move to stablecoins is a move to private issuance, and it is still issuance: a claim on whoever stands behind the token. That matters more in a machine age, not less. If thought is cheap and anything digital can be copied, the one digital thing that must not be copyable is money. Whoever is trusted to keep it scarce then holds the position that the previous section argued nobody can fill well, which is the planner's.

The alternative is a base whose scarcity needs nobody's word. Gold was one. It is scarce by nature and nobody's promise, but it is heavy and hard to test, so people held claims on it instead. A digital base has to get its scarcity from a rule, and the difficulty is to have a rule without a ruler.

The construction that solved it (Nakamoto, 2008) can be stated briefly. Transactions are put in a single public order by chaining each batch of records to the one before, so that changing an old entry would mean changing every later one (Haber and Stornetta, 1991). Adding a batch requires a computation that is costly to perform and trivial to check, so rewriting the past would mean redoing that work faster than everyone else combined. New units are issued with each batch on a schedule fixed in advance and capped at twenty-one million, and anyone can verify the whole record for himself. A holder who keeps his own keys then has no counterparty.

The energy of the previous section buys three things here. It sets the cost of rewriting the record. It makes new units costly to obtain, so nobody issues for free. And it shows that real resources were committed to this history and no other. What it does not do is govern supply. Ford's energy dollar was a claim on energy, so abundant energy would have meant abundant money. Here cheaper energy raises the work done per batch and leaves the schedule alone.

"Trustless" needs a careful reading. The design does not remove assumptions. It moves trust from persons and institutions to three other things: that certain mathematical problems are hard, that no single party controls most of the work, and that users keep running the rule. A rule announced in advance is not a command. But rules age, and changing one is a collective decision that no code can make.

## 6. Scarce is not yet valuable

Building a scarce base does not make it valuable. Value comes from wants, and that holds for scarcity made by rule as for anything else. Scarcity is necessary for value and not sufficient. Menger required four things of a good: a human need, a causal link to satisfying it, knowledge of that link, and command over the thing. A capped supply meets the last. The first three depend on other people wanting to hold it.

The pricing rule is simple. When supply is fixed, the price settles where the existing stock equals the total demand to hold it (Rothbard, 1962). The supply curve of a rule-fixed unit is vertical by construction, so its price is demand and nothing else.

Demand for money looks circular. People hold it because it has purchasing power, and it has purchasing power because people hold it. Mises (1912) broke the circle with history. Today's demand rests on yesterday's purchasing power, and the chain runs back to a commodity that was once valued for its use, as gold was for ornament before it was money. This is the regression theorem.

The modern literature faces the same circularity and concludes that many outcomes are possible, including a price of zero (Garratt and Wallace, 2018; Biais et al., 2023; Pagnotta, 2022). The two views are closer than they look. In both, nothing in the asset fixes its price. History does the selecting: people expect tomorrow's price to be near today's, so the past picks one path out of many. That is not the market choosing the best money. It is convention settling on one, and a convention can hold for a very long time or break.

The new unit tested the strict version of the theorem. It was never a commodity. Nobody wore it, ate it or used it in industry before it was traded, so on a strict reading it should never have acquired a price. It did. Davidson and Block (2015) argue that the commodity requirement binds only when money first emerges from barter, and this unit was born into a world that already had money prices. Luther (2018) argues that the case shows the theorem is wrong or narrow.

One historical detail favours a middle reading. The first published exchange rate, in October 2009, was computed from the cost of electricity. Cost supplied a focal point for the first trade; demand took over afterwards. The requirement of a commodity origin did not hold, but the role of history did.

For the argument of this article the consequence is the one this section began with. A rule can secure the supply. It says nothing about demand, so theory provides no floor under the price and no ceiling over it.

Hayek predicted in 1976 that if people could choose their money, they would choose stable purchasing power. The market has run the experiment. Dollar-pegged tokens stood at roughly $285–290 billion in mid-November 2026 ([DefiLlama](https://defillama.com/stablecoins)). They exist to be spent, not held for gain. So the demand for a stable private medium was met by a tokenised dollar, and the fixed-supply unit kept the demand for savings. Its design is a fixed stock indifferent to the price level, not Hayek's managed private money. And each token is a promise by an issuer to redeem, which returns the argument to trust.

## 7. Trust returns with every promise

Only the base asset held with one's own keys has no counterparty. Everything built on it is a claim, enforced by law, and the market price of each claim is a running measure of how far it is trusted. A share in a company that holds the asset, a bond issued against it, a loan secured by it, a fund unit and a balance at a custodian are all promises of this kind, and each rests on a court and a balance sheet. Trust in the base is bought with time, by waiting for settlement to become final. Trust in a claim is bought with yield.

That is why law came back. Dai (1998) hoped that contracts among pseudonyms could be enforced by posted bonds and reputation. Reputation works inside small closed groups, and impersonal exchange needs a third party who can compel (Greif, 2006). Code settles. It does not adjudicate.

So what do status-seeking, credit-using people do with a base that cannot be inflated? They do three things, and together they show that a scarce base removes neither credit nor state money nor rivalry. What it changes is who bears the losses.

**They lend against it.** The demand for credit comes from differences in patience, not from the monetary base, and a technology that permits full reserves does not make anyone choose them. Within fifteen years a lending market had formed on the new base, and in 2022 it had its first crisis. Several large lenders had taken customers' holdings into custody and lent them on, and when the loans went bad they could not give the holdings back. What happened next is the instructive part. Nobody could create new units to rescue them. The lenders went bankrupt, their creditors took the losses, and holders who had lent nothing lost nothing. A hard base did not prevent the credit cycle. It changed who paid for it.

**They keep their accounts in something else.** Credit on the new base is secured by it and denominated in state money. Few borrowers will promise to repay an asset they expect to appreciate, and people quote prices in the unit their wages and debts are already in (Doepke and Schneider, 2017). The base therefore works as savings and as collateral inside the existing price system. It is not a unit of account. That sets the limit of what it protects. It shelters savings from whoever manages the money, which today means the central bank. It does not shelter the price system.

**They compete with it.** The abundance forecast is not new. Keynes made it in 1930, expecting the economic problem to be solved within a century and the working week to fall to fifteen hours. He was roughly right about productive capacity and wrong about the hours. As material goods got cheaper, people spent a larger share of income bidding for what cannot be multiplied: the flat near the good school, the seat, the view. A scarce asset is natural collateral for that contest, because it lets the owner bid without selling.

New credit always reaches some hands first, a point as old as Cantillon (1755), and in collateralised lending those hands belong to the owners of collateral. That holds whether the credit comes from a central bank or from a private lender. Borrowing against a trustless base does not abolish the channel. It adds the holders of the base to it.

## 8. Machines, work and who owns the capital

The remaining question is who ends up owning the scarce things. If machines make labour reproducible, income shifts to whoever owns what machines cannot make.

For a task a machine can do, competition pushes the price toward the cost of the electricity and hardware time it uses, and that cost becomes a ceiling on what a person can charge for the same task (Hanson, 2016; Korinek and Suh, 2024). This is not the end of work. Comparative advantage still holds: even if machines are better at everything, people keep a role while machine capacity is scarce and has better uses, and it is scarce while energy and computing power are. The tasks that stay outside the equation are those where the buyer is paying for a person, such as care, teaching, craft, performance and accountability (Baumol, 1967). But nothing in the theory keeps any particular wage above any particular level, or makes the adjustment quick. British output per worker rose for about forty years from 1800 while real wages stayed nearly flat (Allen, 2009). Forty years is a working life.

The abundance forecast skips the question of who owns the machines, and it is the old question of who owns the means of production. Money stops mattering to consumers only if the output reaches them. It can reach them through shareholding, which rewards those who saved and bore the risk and is defensible only while entry stays open. It can reach them through redistribution by the state, which is a scheme run by whoever controls the machines and brings back the planner. Or it can follow collateral, so that whoever can borrow against scarce assets acquires the machines. The politics of the coming decades will be fought between these answers, and a bearer asset outside every issuer's control is the hedge for those who doubt whichever planner wins.

## 9. Where this leaves us

This week supplied a small example of what cheap thought looks like. On 20 November 2026 Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, released a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, open since 1859, that he had found working with an AI model. It runs to about 17 million lines of formal code. Within a day the Lean and Mathlib communities had certified it with independent checking programs ([joint statement](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/)), and no person has yet read all of it ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/21/riemann-hypothesis-has-the-worlds-most-famous-maths-problem-been-cracked)). The proof was costly to produce and cheap to check, so it could be believed without anyone being trusted. Understanding it is another matter, and that is rationed by human hours.

More interesting than the proof is what follows it. The next results will be built by machines on what machines have proved, checked by machines, and handed to us afterwards. Each becomes an input to the next at the cost of electricity. Thought is then a produced good that helps to produce itself, and the pace is no longer set by how fast people can read.

A piece of speculative fiction that circulated online in the summer imagined that sequence, and guessed this week for its first step. In the story the results arrive faster and grow stranger, until the last item on the list is not a result at all. It is a song.

What would such a song be? Everything machines have made so far is a means. A proof, a design or a diagnosis is wanted for the sake of something else, and what it is wanted for is still decided by people. That is why value could be traced back to human wants in the first place. The song would be the first product that is not a means. It would work on the wants themselves.

What would it sound like? Not like the proof. The proof is seventeen million lines that nobody has read, believed because it can be checked. The song would be the reverse: taken in whole, by anyone, in minutes, and impossible to check, because nothing in it is true or false. Its effect on the listener would be the only evidence there is.

And what would it do to us? It would go after the one thing this article found scarce in every case, which is the hour. Something composed by a system that knows what moves people better than they know it themselves would be the strongest claim on attention ever made. If preferences show themselves only in choice, then whoever can steer choice has no need to plan the economy. The planner returns as a composer of wants. A world in which everyone wants the same one thing is the other road to the world of the first section, where nothing is ranked above anything else.

This is speculation, and nothing in economics settles it. But the old answer to such a song is worth recalling. Odysseus wanted to hear the Sirens and knew what hearing would do to his judgment, so he had himself tied to the mast beforehand (Elster, 1979). He did not rely on his future self. He relied on a knot. A rule fixed in advance that nobody can change is a mast. That may be the strongest reason to care whether money can rest on a rule instead of on someone's discretion: not that machines will make things cheap, but that they may become very good at changing minds. The knot has its own weakness. It holds only as long as certain mathematical problems stay hard, and machines that prove theorems faster than people can read them are the ones that will test it.

Elon Musk, who often cites the Culture novels, said in 2026 that money will not matter by 2036 ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/07/27/elon-musk-post-money-ai-abundance-future-vinod-khosla-dystopia/)). His reasoning is that people want money for goods and services, and that robots will supply more of both than anyone can use. He also grants that electricity and mass will stay constrained ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/latest/trends/story/elon-musk-says-robots-will-kill-jobs-and-cash-are-you-ready-for-whats-coming-next-502862-2025-11-20)).

Whether he is right depends on questions this article can frame but not settle. Will anything remain that people rank and cannot all have? If so, who decides how it is used: a planner whom everyone must trust, or prices? If prices, stated in what money, and resting on whose promise? And who will own what stays scarce?

If every answer goes the Culture's way, money will not matter. If one of them does not, it will. Either way the day will still have twenty-four hours, and each of us will still be deciding what to do first. That is all scarcity ever meant.
